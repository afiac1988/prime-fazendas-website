How to evaluate a farm before buying it

The seven points that separate a good acquisition from years of trouble — from soil to title.

Buying a farm is not buying hectares. It is buying productive capacity, legal security and future liquidity. Two neighboring areas, the same size and the same price, can have completely different real value. The difference lies in what you look at before signing.

1. Soil and real suitability, not the advertised kind

Fertility, depth, texture and correction history define what the area actually delivers. Soil analysis at representative points and yield history from recent harvests say more than any listing description. "Prime" land with no track record is a promise; with a track record, it's an asset.

2. Water

Water availability sets the ceiling for productivity and the possibility of irrigation. It is worth mapping springs, perennial streams, existing wells and — a point that often gets overlooked — whether there is a water use permit (outorga) for the intended use.

3. Topography and mechanization

Slope determines whether the area is mechanizable, what the operating cost will be, and how much of the total area is actually usable. Total area and usable area rarely coincide.

4. Logistics

Distance to the warehouse, the port and the input supplier goes straight into the freight bill for every harvest, every year. A road that is passable during the rainy season is infrastructure, not a detail.

5. Documentation — where most deals get stuck

This is the item that destroys the most value when neglected:

  • An individualized, up-to-date title deed, free of liens
  • Georeferencing approved by INCRA (Brazil's land reform agency)
  • An active, validated CAR (Rural Environmental Registry), with the legal reserve defined
  • CCIR and ITR (rural property registration and tax) up to date
  • Negative certificates from the seller, including labor and tax matters
  • Verification of overlap with indigenous land, quilombola territory, a conservation unit or a settlement area

Documentation issues are not just a risk: they are a price discount and a block on bank financing.

6. Environmental liabilities

Irregular deforestation, an IBAMA (federal environmental agency) embargo, a state fine, or a legal-reserve deficit travel with the property, not with the previous seller. Checking public embargo databases before making an offer is mandatory.

7. Price per usable hectare

Always compare by usable hectare, adjusting for existing infrastructure, logistics distance and document regularity. It is the only number that lets you compare different opportunities honestly.

Rule of thumb: if the seller cannot deliver the title, the CAR and the georeferencing within a week, the problem isn't the paperwork. It's the property.

The role of advisory services

None of these seven points require the buyer to be a specialist — they require someone competent and independent to look on their behalf. That is exactly what we do: technical, legal and environmental due diligence before you commit capital.
