Lease or buy land: doing the math right

The two decisions answer different questions. Confusing them is costly on both sides.

"Is it better to lease or to buy?" is the question we hear most — and it is poorly framed. Leasing and buying do not compete with each other: they solve different problems. Whoever treats them as alternatives in the same equation usually gets it wrong.

What each one actually delivers

Leasing buys productive capacity. You pay for the right to produce on an area for a set term. Capital stays free for machinery, inputs and operating costs — which is where operational return happens.

Buying buys the asset. You pay for the land itself, and you start capturing its appreciation. But you tie up capital that would otherwise flow into the operation.

These are distinct theses. A producer who wants to scale fast with the capital on hand leases. An investor who wants a real asset and wealth protection buys. A well-capitalized producer who wants both does both.

The math that usually goes wrong

The classic mistake is comparing the lease payment with the "income" from owned land, forgetting three things:

  1. The opportunity cost of capital. Buying 1,000 hectares ties up an amount that, if invested elsewhere, would yield a return. That forgone yield is a cost of buying and needs to enter the equation.
  1. Appreciation is not income. It is real, but it only becomes cash when you sell or pledge the land as collateral. It does not pay the fertilizer bill in May.
  1. Risk shifts, it doesn't disappear. With leasing you carry renewal and adjustment risk. With buying, liquidity risk: land does not sell in 30 days.

An honest way to compare

Put both scenarios on the same horizon — 10 years is usually reasonable — and compare final net worth, not the year's result:

  • Leasing: annual operating result, minus the lease payment, with the freed-up capital invested or reinvested in the operation.
  • Buying: annual operating result without a lease payment, minus the opportunity cost of the tied-up capital, plus the accumulated appreciation of the land.

Done this way, the comparison almost always reveals that the answer depends on two variables: how much capital you have and how long you plan to stay. Short horizons and scarce capital favor leasing. Long horizons and available capital favor buying.

Where document regularity comes in

It matters on both ends, and it is underrated on both:

  • Leasing, a contract over an area with a pending registration issue can be challenged. You invest in soil correction on land whose possession is disputable — and you lose the improvement along with the contract.
  • Buying, we've covered this already: an irregular area does not qualify for financing and drags out closing.

In both cases, checking the title, the CAR and the georeferencing before signing costs a fraction of what it costs to find out afterward.

There is no generic answer. There is your own math, with your own capital, on your own timeline.
