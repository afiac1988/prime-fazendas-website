ESG and carbon credits in land value

Separating what already affects price today from what is still a promise — and what a property needs to enter that conversation.

Few subjects have generated as much noise in the land market as ESG and carbon credits. There is legitimate enthusiasm, and there is promise sold as certainty. It's worth separating the two.

What already affects price today

Environmental regularity. This is the concrete, immediate part. A property with a validated CAR, a defined legal reserve and zero liability accesses credit, attracts institutional buyers and closes faster — like Fazenda Amazonita, with documented environmental certification. An area with an embargo or a reserve deficit drops off the radar of most qualified buyers — and that shows up in the price.

Supply chain traceability. Commodity buyers have started requiring proof of origin. A farm that cannot prove compliance has fewer sales channels, and fewer channels means less negotiating power.

Access to credit on better terms. Credit lines with an environmental counterpart exist and are expanding. They are not philanthropy: they require proof, and whoever doesn't have their documentation in order doesn't get access.

What is still a promise

Carbon credits as guaranteed income. Here, honesty is required. The voluntary carbon market is real, but it has characteristics that tend to be left out of sales pitches:

  • Volatile price. The value per ton fluctuates significantly and depends on methodology, the certifier and demand from the end buyer.
  • High entry cost. Certification, verification and monitoring are expensive and require scale.
  • Long timeline. Serious projects take years between structuring and the first issuance.
  • Additionality. Serious methodologies require proving that the conservation would not happen anyway. An area that was already preserved and would remain preserved may not generate credits.

None of this invalidates the thesis. It invalidates the version where you buy a piece of cerrado today and it pays for itself with carbon next year.

How to evaluate an opportunity that promises carbon

Four questions that separate a real project from a pitch:

  1. What is the methodology and who is the certifier? If the answer is vague, the project doesn't exist yet.
  2. Who pays for structuring and verification? It's a real, recurring cost, not a detail.
  3. Is there an identified buyer? Credits issued with no buyer are inventory, not revenue.
  4. Does the additionality hold up? If the area is a mandatory legal reserve, it will hardly generate credit — preserving it is already a legal duty.

The point that matters to a land buyer

Our take is straightforward: buy the farm for what it produces and for the documentation it has. If an environmental project comes along later, great — it's an added gain on top of an asset that already stood on its own.

The reverse path — paying a premium betting on future environmental revenue — is speculation with a nice name.

Environmental regularity is the asset. A carbon credit is a possibility that regularity opens up.

What the property needs to have

To enter any serious conversation about environmental assets:

  • An active, validated CAR consistent with the title
  • A defined legal reserve, with no deficit
  • No federal or state embargo
  • Approved georeferencing
  • A documented land-use history

It's the same list that makes a farm negotiable in the first place. Not by coincidence: both things measure the same quality.
